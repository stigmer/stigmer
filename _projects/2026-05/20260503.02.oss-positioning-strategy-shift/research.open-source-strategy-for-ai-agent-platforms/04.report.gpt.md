# Optimal Open-Source Strategy for Stigmer

## Executive Summary

Stigmer should **keep its core platform under Apache 2.0 and move from “cloud-primary, OSS as trust signal” to an “OSS-led discovery, cloud-led monetization” strategy**. The strongest analogues for your product are Temporal, Supabase, PostHog, LangChain/LangSmith, GitLab, and Grafana: all show that developer-facing infrastructure wins adoption when the open product is easy to inspect, run, and build on, while the commercial business is built on operating it for teams at scale with security, compliance, collaboration, and support. The newest large-sample venture data is also favorable: Linux Foundation/COSSA/Serena’s 2025 study found commercial open-source companies outperform comparable closed-source peers on valuations, funding speed, and exits, especially in infrastructure software, and that stronger community health correlates with stronger company outcomes. citeturn7search4turn7search0turn8search3turn10search0turn18search5turn37search0turn37search11

The highest-confidence recommendations are these:

- **Keep Apache 2.0 for the main `stigmer` repo for now.** At your stage, adoption, trust, and ecosystem position matter more than anti-cloud-competitor protection. HashiCorp’s BUSL shift triggered OpenTofu; Redis’ CEO later said the SSPL move achieved its anti-hyperscaler goal but hurt community relations. That is a bad trade for an early category-creation company that still needs mindshare. citeturn13search12turn13search0turn13search5turn30search2turn27search3

- **Change positioning now, not later.** On technical surfaces—homepage hero, docs, README, architecture pages, conference talks—lead with “open-source AI agent infrastructure” and “self-host or use Stigmer Cloud.” Keep the primary conversion path to cloud, but stop treating open source as a footer-level reassurance. Supabase, Temporal, and PostHog all visibly foreground the open product while also steering serious users toward managed cloud. citeturn8search3turn8search1turn7search6turn7search17turn10search0turn10search2

- **Do not cripple the OSS product.** Your current instinct to keep the self-hosted product genuinely functional is more right than wrong. ## Case Studies

### Temporal

Temporal is the cleanest technical analogue. Its core service is open source and self-hostable; its docs explicitly describe self-hosting the open-source Temporal Service, while positioning Temporal Cloud as the easiest way to run it and pricing it as a consumption service. In March 2025 the company said it had more than 2,500 enterprise customers on Temporal Cloud and had achieved 4.4x revenue growth over the prior period, showing that a durable-execution infrastructure company can keep the engine open and still build a serious cloud business. The lesson for Stigmer is that **durable orchestration is exactly the sort of infra where teams often start self-hosted and later pay to stop operating it themselves**. citeturn7search0turn7search5turn7search17turn2search16turn7search3

### Supabase

Supabase positions open source as part of the brand, not as a footnote. Its homepage says it is “built in the open,” its docs strongly support self-hosting, and its GitHub repository shows very large community gravity with roughly 102k stars. At the same time, Supabase keeps the hosted product front and center, and private-market analysts estimated it reached about $70M ARR in 2025. The lesson is that **a fully usable self-hosted product can still be an enormous top-of-funnel asset for cloud, provided the hosted experience is faster, more integrated, and more operationally complete**. citeturn8search3turn8search1turn8search7turn9view0turn2search17

### PostHog

PostHog is especially informative because it has lived both sides of the question. Its OSS deployment remains MIT licensed and self-hostable, but its FAQ plainly recommends PostHog Cloud for most teams and emphasizes that self-hosted users miss continuously shipped improvements. At the same time, PostHog still talks openly about its open-source model and, in 2026, a public podcast episode described the company as having recently passed $100M ARR. The lesson is not “open source loses”; it is that **open source drives adoption, but cloud must become the obvious operating choice once teams become serious**. citeturn10search2turn10search0turn10search14turn11search0turn10search8

### Sentry

Sentry is useful because it sits between open-source ethos and commercial pragmatism. It now licenses Sentry and Codecov under the Functional Source License and argues publicly for delayed open source / fair source; its licensing page also says it aims to deliver the same functionality in self-hosted and cloud offerings. That is a clear statement of philosophy: the monetization boundary is not “cripple the product,” but rather “preserve the company’s ability to keep building it.” The lesson for Stigmer is that fair-source can work, but it changes the trust profile: it is a deliberate trade of some community purity for anti-free-riding protection. citeturn12search2turn12search6turn12search8turn12search1

### HashiCorp

HashiCorp’s BUSL switch is the canonical cautionary tale. The company said it adopted BSL to ensure continued investment and better protect against competitive hosted services. The immediate external result was OpenTofu: the Linux Foundation-backed fork created specifically in response to the BUSL move. HashiCorp may have improved its formal legal protection, but it also surrendered a large amount of symbolic and governance capital. For Stigmer, the lesson is simple: **license hardening is least damaging when done very early or on net-new code; changing the rules after developer trust has formed is expensive**. citeturn13search12turn13search0turn13search5turn13search1turn13search2

### GitLab

GitLab is the classic open-core success case. It has long been explicit that GitLab CE is open and EE contains proprietary features, and its self-managed feature-comparison pages make the tier boundary legible. It also runs one of the largest transparent contributor programs in software; its contributor platform says it has 4,000+ contributors, and GitLab’s FY2025 annual report recorded $759M in revenue. The lesson for Stigmer is that open core can scale massively, but it imposes a permanent packaging discipline: **you must always be able to explain why a feature belongs in core, premium, or ultimate**. citeturn15search3turn15search15turn15search10turn6view0

### Grafana Labs

Grafana moved its core projects to AGPL in 2021 while keeping plugins, agents, and some other pieces Apache licensed. That did not stop commercial scale: in 2025 Grafana Labs announced it had surpassed $400M ARR and 7,000 customers. The lesson is that strong copyleft can coexist with a very large observability business when the product is already deeply embedded and operationally central. But for Stigmer, the relevant nuance is that **observability dashboards are easier to isolate from customer application code than an agent platform intended to be embedded into product workflows and called through SDKs and APIs**. citeturn16search0turn3search10turn4search1turn16search9

### n8n

n8n is a near-neighbor on workflow automation. It is not OSI open source; its docs explicitly say that because of the Sustainable Use License it does not call itself open source, even though source is available and self-hosting is free. That position has still produced huge adoption—its GitHub repo shows about 186k stars—and in its 2025 Series B announcement the company said ARR had grown 5x in the prior year. The lesson for Stigmer is that source-available models can absolutely work commercially in automation, but they are a different brand promise from Apache or MIT. If you want to become the neutral platform standard for AI agents, n8n’s licensing posture is not the cleanest precedent. citeturn17search0turn17search4turn17search1turn17search3

### LangChain and LangSmith

LangChain is the most important domain example. Its OSS layer is very open—LangChain and LangGraph are open-source frameworks, with LangGraph explicitly highlighting durable execution, persistence, and human-in-the-loop support—while LangSmith is a paid platform for observing, evaluating, and deploying agents. TechCrunch reported LangSmith had driven LangChain to roughly $12M–$16M ARR by mid-2025. The lesson is that in agent infrastructure, **open-source frameworks win developer mindshare, while commercial value clusters around reliability, eval, deployment, operations, and team workflows**. That is the strongest public evidence for how Stigmer should monetize without closing the core. citeturn18search5turn18search1turn18search8turn18search0turn18search19turn18search3turn19search0turn19search1

### CrewAI

CrewAI is the closest direct peer in narrative, but still more framework-leaning than platform-leaning. Its OSS core is MIT licensed, its repo shows roughly 50.5k stars, and its README markets a commercial “AMP Suite” / control plane with observability, centralized management, security, support, and both cloud and on-prem deployment options. That is effectively the exact commercial boundary Stigmer should favor: **keep agent construction and orchestration open; sell administration, operational scale, and enterprise controls**. citeturn20view0turn18search2

For a platform like Stigmer, the real question is not “which license is most protective?” but “what is the highest-value asset right now?” In your current phase, the asset is not the code alone. It is **developer trust, category definition, ecosystem gravity, and de facto standard status around the resource model, APIs, SDKs, and deployment patterns**. Apache 2.0 is best aligned with building that asset. citeturn28search0turn37search5turn37search11

### The cloud-competitor risk

The cloud-competitor problem is real, but historically it has shown up most aggressively in categories with three traits: **very large managed-service demand**, **clear operational boundaries**, and **broad developer familiarity**. Search and observability triggered the Elastic/AWS conflict; document databases drove MongoDB vs. Amazon DocumentDB; Redis’ 2024 licensing change quickly catalyzed Valkey and new hyperscaler support. Those are all massive, obvious managed-service categories. citeturn30search4turn30search0turn30search1turn30search5turn30search2turn30search10

The AI agent market is large and fast-growing, and hyperscalers are clearly moving into it—AWS now offers Bedrock Agents and AgentCore, Google offers Vertex AI Agent Builder and Agent Engine, and CB Insights describes Google, Microsoft, and Amazon as actively carving up the AI agent market. But that does **not** mean a hyperscaler is likely to copy and commoditize an early-stage OSS agent platform in the same way it did a document database or search engine. Today’s agent platforms are still immature, change rapidly, and are differentiated as much by orchestration semantics, integrations, safety/approval flows, and developer UX as by runtime code. That lowers the immediate “take your Apache repo and relaunch it as a big managed cloud service” risk relative to the classic database/search cases. citeturn21search0turn21search12turn21search1turn21search13turn32search2turn32search1turn32search5

My judgment is that hyperscaler risk for Stigmer is **non-zero but presently secondary**. The more likely risks over the next 12–24 months are: larger model/platform vendors launching adjacent managed capabilities; framework companies expanding “up” into platform features; and proprietary agent builders bundling similar workflows into broader AI suites. Those are go-to-market and ecosystem risks more than raw license-exploitation risks. citeturn21search12turn21search13turn18search8turn18search12turn21search0

### License recommendation

The highest-confidence recommendation is:

- **Keep Apache 2.0 for the current OSS repo**
- **Do not relicense the core now**
- **If protection becomes necessary later, apply it only to new commercial control-plane/ops layers, not to the existing open core** citeturn28search0turn13search5turn30search2turn12search6

Apache is the right fit because Stigmer wants to be trusted as neutral infrastructure, wants public protobuf contracts and SDK generation to be a moat, and serves engineering teams who will compare it directly with OSS frameworks and proprietary cloud agent services. AGPL would likely introduce unnecessary legal friction into app teams embedding agents into products. SSPL and Elastic-style licenses would damage credibility in a market where openness is still rare and valuable. BSL/FSL remain possible future tools, but today they would solve the wrong problem and likely slow the right flywheel. citeturn29search3turn27search3turn29search1turn28search3turn18search5turn20view0

## Positioning Strategy

The evidence does not support keeping open source as a mere trust badge. For developer infrastructure, **open source works best as the discovery layer and trust architecture**, while cloud is the easiest buying path once a team decides to productionize. Supabase literally leads with “built in the open.” Temporal’s homepage calls itself an open-source durable execution platform while still pushing cloud. PostHog continues to foreground its self-hostability and OSS roots even as it explicitly recommends cloud for most teams. citeturn8search3turn7search6turn7search17turn10search0turn10search2

That suggests three important conclusions for Stigmer:

### Open source should be the hero on technical channels

GitHub, README, docs, launch posts, conference talks, architecture pages, and comparative content are where your buyer first decides whether Stigmer is “real infrastructure” or “just another agent wrapper.” On those surfaces, the winning message is some version of: **“Stigmer is the open-source platform for durable, governed AI agents. Self-host it, inspect it, or use Stigmer Cloud.”** That framing emphasizes inspectability, portability, and seriousness—the things your anti-personas care about. citeturn8search7turn7search4turn18search5turn21search13

### Cloud should remain the default conversion path

This is not a recommendation to bury cloud. The evidence from PostHog, Temporal, and Supabase is that the commercial surface should still make cloud the easiest path to value: fastest signup, hosted identity, usage-based plans, managed upgrades, integrated secrets, hosted observability, and production guardrails. The mistake is not being cloud-commercial; the mistake is making the open product look secondary or cosmetic. citeturn10search0turn7search1turn8search3turn12search4

### The correct answer is a hybrid, not a binary

For Stigmer, the best positioning is: **OSS-first in narrative, cloud-first in checkout**. In plain language:

- **Homepage hero and developer docs:** open-source platform first
- **Pricing and signup flows:** managed cloud first
- **README and local quickstart:** frictionless self-host and local dev
- **Enterprise pages:** governance, security, and support
- **Comparison pages:** “why Stigmer vs frameworks / proprietary builders / hand-rolled agent infra” citeturn8search3turn7search17turn10search0turn18search12

This hybrid is also what best fits your actual product. Based on the architecture in your brief, you already have the ingredients that technical buyers associate with “real platform,” not “toy OSS”: a declarative resource model, public API contracts, SDK generation, durable execution, MCP alignment, and workflow semantics. You should market those as first-order reasons to adopt. 

## Community Building

The companies that convert OSS traction into business value do not wait for “community” to appear spontaneously. They construct a **contributor ladder** and a **user-to-customer path**. The newest commercial OSS research reinforces why that matters: stronger community health is associated with stronger company outcomes, and VC-backed commercial OSS projects tend to see more contributors and dependent projects after funding. citeturn37search0turn37search5turn37search11

The playbook most applicable to Stigmer is:

### Build the easiest contribution surfaces first

For Stigmer, the best first contribution categories are not the scheduler or persistence engine. They are:

- MCP server integrations and examples
- SDK examples and starter apps
- YAML resource templates
- approval-flow patterns
- docs improvements
- language client improvements
- deployment recipes
- connector/tooling packs

That is how Supabase, GitLab, Grafana, and n8n widen the funnel: their public repos, community channels, examples, and extension points make it possible to contribute before someone is ready to modify the kernel. citeturn9view0turn15search10turn16search6turn17search14

### Favor GitHub Discussions plus one real-time community channel

For infrastructure products, the best stack is usually **GitHub Issues for bugs**, **GitHub Discussions for design/Q&A**, and **Discord or Slack for real-time help/community**. Supabase explicitly surfaces a community forum and Discord; Sentry uses GitHub Discussions for launch feedback and product discussion; GitLab formalizes contribution through its contributor platform. citeturn9view0turn12search5turn15search10

### Make documentation a growth product, not a support artifact

PostHog, Temporal, Supabase, LangChain, and Microsoft’s Semantic Kernel all invest heavily in task-first docs. For Stigmer, the golden-path docs should be built around buying moments, not architecture categories:

- Build your first agent from YAML and API
- Add private domain knowledge
- Connect an MCP tool safely
- Add a human approval checkpoint
- Run a multi-step workflow that survives failure
- Move from local self-host to cloud without rewrites

That kind of docs-led growth is especially important because your buyers are technical founders and engineering leads, not hobbyists. citeturn7search4turn8search1turn18search5turn21search6

### Measure metrics that sit near commercial intent

GitHub stars matter, but mainly as top-of-funnel proof of developer attention. The stronger indicators for Stigmer will be: Docker pulls, docs traffic to setup and deployment pages, API key signups from docs, self-host installs, number of production-like sessions/workflows, number of organizations using approval policies, external contributions merged, number of MCP/tool integrations, and cloud upgrades coming from self-hosted or local users. Linux Foundation’s 2025 report is especially useful here: it links community health more strongly to company outcomes than vanity metrics alone. citeturn37search0turn37search5turn26search6

### Treat contributors as future design partners, not free labor

The best OSS companies publicly recognize contributors and create ladders of status. GitLab runs recognition systems for contributors; Grafana has its Champions program; n8n has increasingly embraced community nodes; Supabase and Letta both highlight community channels prominently. Stigmer should do the same: public roadmap, contributor badges, integration spotlights, community demos, and “built with Stigmer” showcases. citeturn15search2turn16search6turn17search14turn25search18

# Executive Summary

## Top recommendations

### 1. Switch from “cloud-primary, OSS as trust signal” to **OSS-led hybrid positioning**

For Stigmer, open source is not just reassurance. It is the distribution channel, the standardization strategy, the developer trust mechanism, and the ecosystem foundation. Infrastructure buyers evaluate through code, docs, GitHub, CLI, SDKs, and local tests before they talk to sales. That is especially true for an AI agent platform that will touch tools, permissions, memory, approvals, workflows, and sensitive enterprise data.

The right market posture is:

> **“Stigmer is the open-source agent infrastructure platform for engineering teams. Run it locally or self-host it. Use Stigmer Cloud when you want production-grade operations, governance, security, and scale.”**

This is not the same as becoming an OSS charity project. It is the Temporal/Supabase/GitLab/PostHog-style hybrid: developers start with open source; teams pay when production operations, compliance, governance, scale, or support become painful.

The timing matters. Gartner expects enterprise agentic AI adoption to rise sharply, including task-specific agents inside enterprise applications, while also warning about agent sprawl, governance, and MCP security incidents. That makes “inspectable, portable, governable agent infrastructure” a strong wedge right now. ([Gartner][1])

### 2. Keep the core under **Apache 2.0** for now

Do not move Stigmer core to BSL, FSL, SSPL, Elastic License, or Confluent-style source-available licensing at this stage. The biggest early-stage risk is not AWS copying you. The biggest risk is irrelevance.

Apache 2.0 gives you the strongest combination of developer trust, enterprise procurement comfort, patent protection, contribution friendliness, and compatibility with the open standards story around MCP, protobuf contracts, SDKs, and YAML resource definitions. Apache 2.0 is permissive, OSI-approved, and includes express copyright and patent license terms. ([Apache Software Foundation][2])

Your “genuinely open source” claim is strategically valuable in the AI-agent platform market. Do not voluntarily give it up before you have enough adoption to protect.

### 3. Keep the OSS product fully functional

The fact that Stigmer OSS is already a real, self-hostable product is not a mistake. It is your strongest adoption asset.

Keep open:

| Should remain open               | Why                                           |
| -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Agent resource model             | Creates the standard developers learn         |
| Skills/domain-knowledge format   | Enables ecosystem and portability             |
| MCP tool integration             | Aligns with an emerging open standard         |
| Human approval flows             | Core differentiator; must be inspectable      |
| Workflow DSL integration         | Makes the platform credible as infrastructure |
| Temporal-based runners           | Proves durability and reliability             |
| Public protobuf APIs             | Reduces lock-in fear                          |
| CLI, SDKs, console               | Enables developer-led adoption                |
| SQLite/local-first control plane | Makes evaluation instant                      |
| Docs, examples, templates        | Drives community and SEO                      |

Do not put the foundational primitives behind a paywall. Monetize the operational, organizational, and governance layers around them.

### 4. Commercialize **production operations, enterprise governance, and managed infrastructure**

The correct commercial line is not “agents are free, approval flows are paid” or “MCP is free, workflows are paid.” That would damage trust and adoption.

The correct commercial line is:

| Commercial / Cloud / Enterprise           | Rationale                                         |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Managed HA control plane                  | Teams do not want to operate agent infrastructure |
| Multi-tenant organizations                | Business/team need, not individual developer need |
| SSO/SAML/OIDC/SCIM                        | Enterprise buyer value                            |
| Advanced RBAC/OpenFGA                     | Governance and compliance value                   |
| Audit logs                                | Compliance and security value                     |
| Approval routing and policy governance    | Enterprise-scale HITL                             |
| Managed Temporal/Mongo/Redis/S3           | Operational burden removal                        |
| Sandboxed tool execution at scale         | Security-sensitive production capability          |
| Secrets management                        | Enterprise requirement                            |
| Backups, DR, SLA                          | Production operations                             |
| Data residency                            | Enterprise and regulated-market need              |
| Usage analytics and cost controls         | Buyer/admin value                                 |
| Private Skills/MCP registry               | Team/enterprise collaboration                     |
| Verified connectors and security scanning | Trust and ecosystem value                         |
| Support and success                       | Revenue without weakening OSS                     |

This is the GitLab buyer-based open-core logic applied to agent infrastructure: individual developers get the full product primitives; managers, security teams, platform teams, and executives pay for collaboration, control, compliance, and scale. GitLab explicitly frames many paid features around the needs of managers, directors, and executives while keeping major individual-developer features in Free. ([The GitLab Handbook][3])

### 5. Treat cloud competitors as a real long-term risk, but the wrong near-term optimization target

Hyperscaler free-riding is real. MongoDB, Elastic, Redis, and HashiCorp all changed licenses or triggered forks after cloud/provider conflicts. AWS launched MongoDB-compatible DocumentDB, created OpenSearch after Elastic changed licensing, and the Linux Foundation’s Valkey fork emerged after Redis moved away from BSD-style licensing. ([US Press Center][4])

But the pattern is important: hyperscalers usually care once a project becomes a large customer-demanded standard. Stigmer’s immediate problem is not protecting a giant installed base. It is creating one.

The best defense now is to become the trusted open standard for production agent infrastructure before someone else does.

### 6. Build the community around **production agent infrastructure**, not generic AI tinkering

Your anti-personas are correct. Do not compete for hobbyist chatbot demos.

Build community around:

* production agent design patterns,
* MCP server integrations,
* approval-policy recipes,
* domain Skills,
* workflow templates,
* security/sandboxing patterns,
* SDK usage,
* migration from LangChain/LangGraph/CrewAI prototypes into durable production systems.

The strongest community wedge is: **“Bring your prototype agent into production safely.”**

### 7. Reassess licensing later, but do not surprise the community

If a real cloud competitor emerges, or if self-managed Enterprise becomes a meaningful revenue path, you can add proprietary or source-available enterprise modules. But avoid changing the core after the community has formed. HashiCorp’s Terraform BSL move triggered a major OpenTofu fork backed by companies and contributors; Redis’s license change triggered Valkey. ([OpenTofu][5])

If you ever change licensing, do it only for new enterprise modules or with a clear forward-looking policy. Keep SDKs, protobufs, CLI basics, resource definitions, and local core open forever.

# Part 1: Open-Source Business Models for Infrastructure Platforms

## 1. Open Core

### How it works

Open core means the foundational product is open source, while advanced enterprise features are commercial. The free product must be useful enough to drive adoption. Paid features usually map to organizational complexity: SSO, RBAC, audit logs, compliance, advanced administration, scale, premium support, and enterprise governance.

### Examples and scale

| Company      | Model                                                                        | Scale signal                                                                                                                    |
| ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| GitLab       | Open core DevSecOps platform                                                 | Crossed $1B ARR and reported FY2026 revenue of about $955M; subscription revenue was the vast majority. ([about.gitlab.com][6]) |
| PostHog      | Open-source product platform with cloud and enterprise features              | Estimated around $58M ARR in early 2026, with cloud-first growth after originally emphasizing self-hosting. ([Sacra][7])        |
| Sentry       | Started open source, now “fair source” / source-available core with OSS SDKs | Raised at a $3B+ valuation in 2022; broad developer adoption. ([Tech Startups][8])                                              |
| Grafana Labs | OSS/AGPL core plus Enterprise/Cloud                                          | Reported over $400M ARR and 7,000 customers in 2025. ([Grafana Labs][9])                                                        |

### Pros

Open core works well when the product has a natural split between individual developer value and organizational buyer value. Developers can adopt without permission, while managers pay for governance, security, collaboration, and scale.

### Cons

The line between free and paid must be trusted. If core product value feels artificially crippled, developers resent it. If too much enterprise value is free, revenue may lag.

### Fit for Stigmer

Strong fit, but only if implemented carefully. Stigmer should not make agent primitives paid. It should make enterprise operations and governance paid.

**Recommended form:** open core around enterprise governance, not around core agent capability.

### How it works

The software is open source and self-hostable. The company sells a managed cloud service that removes operational burden and adds production-grade reliability, compliance, and scale.

| Company   | Model                                                                     | Scale signal                                                                                                        |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Temporal  | Open-source Durable Execution platform plus Temporal Cloud                | Raised $300M Series D at a $5B valuation in 2026; earlier reported 2,500 Temporal Cloud customers. ([Temporal][10]) |

### Pros

### Cons

The company must make cloud meaningfully better than self-hosting without making OSS fake. Also, cloud margins and reliability become core company capabilities.

### Fit for Stigmer

Very strong. Stigmer’s production burden is real: durable execution, Temporal, sandboxing, secrets, MCP tool access, approvals, memory, traces, policy, and scale. These are exactly the kinds of things teams would rather consume as managed infrastructure.

## 4. Dual Licensing

### How it works

The same code is available under an open-source license and a commercial license. Companies buy the commercial license to avoid copyleft obligations, embed the product in proprietary software, or receive indemnity/support.

### Examples and scale

| Company | Model                                                                   | Scale signal                                  |
| ------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| MySQL   | GPL plus commercial licenses                                            | Sold to Sun for about $1B. ([TechCrunch][16]) |
| MariaDB | GPL heritage plus commercial offerings; also uses BSL for some products | Public-company/commercial database model.     |

### Pros

Works well for embeddable infrastructure where OEMs need proprietary redistribution rights.

### Cons

Requires a copyleft base license or contributor agreements to create leverage. It can add legal friction and reduce casual adoption.

### Fit for Stigmer

Weak to moderate. Stigmer is not primarily an embeddable database or library. It is a platform. Dual licensing would only make sense if you changed the core to AGPL/GPL, which would undermine your adoption strategy.

## 5. Source-Available With Delayed Open Source: BSL/FSL

### How it works

The source code is visible, modifiable, and often free for many uses, but the license restricts certain commercial uses, usually competing hosted services. After a defined period, the code converts to an open-source license.

### Examples

| Company   | License approach                                          | Notes                                                                                                                                             |
| --------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| MariaDB   | Business Source License                                   | Source public; non-production use generally free; converts to open source by a change date, no later than the fourth anniversary. ([MariaDB][17]) |
| HashiCorp | BSL for Terraform/Vault/Consul future releases after 2023 | Changed from MPL to BSL to gain more control over commercialization. ([GlobeNewswire][18])                                                        |
| Sentry    | Functional Source License / Fair Source                   | Converts to Apache 2.0 or MIT after two years; explicitly source-available, not OSI open source. ([TechCrunch][19])                               |
| n8n       | Sustainable Use License / fair-code                       | Allows internal use and modification but restricts hosting/white-label commercial resale; not OSI open source. ([n8n Docs][20])                   |

### Pros

Protects against cloud free-riding while preserving code visibility. More attractive to investors worried about hyperscaler capture.

### Cons

You lose the clean “open source” claim. Community trust is weaker. Some developers and enterprises reject non-OSI licenses. Late changes can trigger forks, as Terraform/OpenTofu showed. ([OpenTofu][5])

### Fit for Stigmer

Not recommended for core now. Potentially useful later for separate self-managed enterprise modules, but only if the product already has adoption and the licensing is communicated clearly.

## 6. AGPL / SSPL Copyleft

### How it works

AGPL is an OSI-approved copyleft license designed for network server software. If someone modifies AGPL software and offers it over a network, they must provide source code for the modified version. ([Open Source Initiative][21])

SSPL goes much further: if someone offers the software as a service, they must release not only modifications but also broad service-management software. MongoDB acknowledges SSPL is not OSI-approved and that MongoDB under SSPL is not considered open source by OSI. ([MongoDB][22])

### Examples

| Company      | License approach                                                                                                                   |
| ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Grafana Labs | Moved Grafana, Loki, and Tempo from Apache 2.0 to AGPLv3 in 2021; kept plugins, agents, and libraries Apache. ([Grafana Labs][23]) |
| MongoDB      | Moved server from AGPL to SSPL to address cloud-service competition. ([MongoDB][24])                                               |

### Pros

AGPL protects against proprietary modified hosted versions while remaining OSI open source. SSPL is stronger against hyperscalers.

### Cons

AGPL creates procurement friction, especially for platform software embedded into commercial products. SSPL is not open source and has poor community perception.

### Fit for Stigmer

AGPL is defensible if Stigmer were primarily a standalone server product. But because Stigmer wants engineering teams to embed agents into products and use SDKs/protobufs widely, AGPL would create anxiety. SSPL should be avoided if “genuine open source” is central to the brand.

## The cloud competitor problem

### Historical pattern

The fear is not imaginary.

MongoDB changed licensing partly because cloud vendors could offer database-as-a-service versions without contributing enough value back. AWS launched DocumentDB as a MongoDB-compatible managed service. ([MongoDB][24])

Elastic changed licensing; AWS responded by creating OpenSearch under Apache 2.0. ([Amazon Web Services, Inc.][44])

Redis moved from BSD-style licensing to source-available licenses in 2024, and a Linux Foundation fork called Valkey emerged with backing from major companies. ([Redis][45])

HashiCorp changed Terraform to BSL, and OpenTofu emerged as a Linux Foundation fork. ([GlobeNewswire][18])

### When hyperscaler risk becomes real

There is no official revenue threshold where AWS, GCP, or Azure decide to compete. But the historical pattern suggests risk rises when:

1. The open-source project becomes a de facto standard.
2. Enterprise customers ask cloud providers for a managed version.
3. The product fits existing hyperscaler infrastructure primitives.
4. API compatibility matters more than brand experience.
5. The original company’s cloud margins become visibly attractive.
6. The project has enough adoption that a fork or compatible clone has immediate demand.

For Stigmer, this suggests:

| Time horizon |         Risk level | Explanation                                                                                            |
| ------------ | -----------------: | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 0–24 months  |    Low to moderate | The market is fragmented; Stigmer needs adoption more than protection.                                 |
| 24–48 months |           Moderate | If Stigmer becomes a known agent-infrastructure standard, large clouds may build compatible offerings. |
| 48+ months   | High if successful | If Stigmer becomes “Terraform/Temporal for agents,” hyperscaler interest becomes plausible.            |

### Does AI agent infrastructure have this risk profile?

Yes, eventually. Agent platforms touch cloud-native workflows, API gateways, IAM, secrets, observability, policy, and data access. These are all areas hyperscalers understand. AWS Bedrock Agents and Google Vertex AI Agent Builder already show that large cloud providers see agents as managed cloud services, not just developer libraries. ([Amazon Web Services, Inc.][46])

But Stigmer has one advantage: the category is still early. Hyperscalers are pushing proprietary ecosystems. That leaves room for an independent open platform to become the trusted neutral layer.

### Recommendation on cloud competitor risk

Do not solve a future hyperscaler problem by weakening today’s adoption story.

Instead, mitigate with:

* trademark protection around “Stigmer,”
* hosted-service terms that prevent misuse of brand,
* Apache 2.0 core plus proprietary Cloud control plane,
* cloud-only enterprise governance features,
* managed infrastructure excellence,
* verified connector/Skills ecosystem,
* security certifications,
* deep integration with Temporal/MCP/protobuf ecosystem,
* community legitimacy.

The code can be copied. The standard, community, brand, hosted reliability, enterprise trust, and ecosystem are harder to copy.

## License recommendation for Stigmer

### Keep Apache 2.0 for the core

Keep Apache 2.0 for:

* control-plane OSS implementation,
* runners,
* public protobufs,
* SDKs,
* CLI,
* local web console,
* YAML resource model,
* Skills format,
* MCP integration layer,
* workflow execution primitives,
* documentation and examples.

This lets Stigmer credibly say:

> **“Stigmer is genuinely open source. Not source-available. Not fair-code. Not open-washing.”**

That sentence matters in the AI-agent platform market because many competitors are either proprietary, framework-only, source-available, or open in only part of the stack.

### Do not switch to AGPL now

AGPL would preserve open-source status and offer some SaaS protection, but it creates uncertainty for companies embedding Stigmer into commercial products. Your primary buyer is an engineering team building software products. Reducing legal friction matters.

### Do not switch to BSL/FSL now

BSL/FSL would weaken your strongest differentiator. They are viable for companies like Sentry or n8n, but those companies made a different tradeoff: fair/source-available sustainability over pure OSS. Stigmer’s category opportunity is to be the real OSS platform.

### Use proprietary licensing for cloud/enterprise modules

Keep `stigmer-cloud` proprietary. If self-managed enterprise becomes important, add a separate `stigmer-enterprise` or `ee/` layer with a commercial license. Do not contaminate the core.

### Add non-license protections

Implement:

* trademark policy,
* contributor policy,
* DCO or lightweight CLA,
* security disclosure policy,
* governance docs,
* clear “open vs. commercial” page,
* explicit promise not to move core primitives behind a paywall.

A DCO is likely more community-friendly than a heavy CLA at this stage. A CLA gives more future relicensing flexibility, but it can reduce contributions. If you have no plan to dual-license core, prefer DCO.

# Part 4: Open-Source Positioning Strategy — Hero vs. Trust Signal

## “Open source as hero” companies

These companies make open source central to their brand, not a footnote.

| Company   | How OSS is used                                                                                              |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Temporal  | “Open-source Durable Execution” is the core identity; Temporal Cloud is managed production. ([Temporal][26]) |
| Supabase  | “Open-source Firebase alternative” is the category hook; cloud is convenience and scale. ([GitHub][28])      |
| GitLab    | Open core and transparent stewardship are core to the brand and buyer trust. ([The GitLab Handbook][3])      |
| Grafana   | Open-source observability identity remains central even after AGPL shift. ([Grafana Labs][23])               |
| LangChain | OSS frameworks drive the ecosystem; LangSmith monetizes the production workflow. ([LangChain][47])           |

### How they do it

They usually have:

* GitHub as a primary acquisition surface,
* docs as a product,
* public roadmap,
* fast local start,
* transparent architecture,
* explicit open/commercial split,
* cloud CTAs inside developer workflows,
* community templates/examples,
* contributor paths.

## “Open source as trust signal” companies

These companies primarily sell cloud/product experience and use open source to reduce buyer anxiety.

| Company type                   | How OSS is used                                                            |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Some hosted developer tools    | “You can inspect/self-host” supports trust, but cloud is the main product  |
| Source-available SaaS          | Code visibility reduces lock-in concern, but license restricts competition |
| Enterprise SaaS with open SDKs | SDKs are OSS, but product is proprietary                                   |

This model can work when the product is mainly a hosted application or when the buyer does not need to deeply evaluate infrastructure internals.

## Which drives more adoption for infrastructure?

For infrastructure, OSS-as-hero generally drives more developer adoption because infrastructure selection is high-trust and high-switching-cost. Developers want to know:

* Can I run it locally?
* What happens if the vendor disappears?
* Can I inspect the failure modes?
* Are the APIs stable?
* Can I automate it?
* Can I integrate it into my stack?
* Can I avoid lock-in?

Commercial open-source research also suggests that community health and open-source adoption are correlated with stronger valuations and funding outcomes for infrastructure companies. The Linux Foundation/COSSA/Serena 2025 report found that commercial open-source software companies outperformed closed-source peers in valuation, funding speed, and liquidity, especially in infrastructure. ([Linux Foundation][48])

## Which drives more revenue?

Open source does not automatically create revenue. It creates adoption, trust, and inbound demand. Revenue comes from packaging the operational pain.

Successful companies convert OSS adoption into revenue by selling:

* hosted cloud,
* enterprise governance,
* compliance,
* support,
* scale,
* collaboration,
* security,
* managed upgrades,
* reliability.

Temporal, Supabase, MongoDB, GitLab, Grafana, Confluent, and Elastic all show that open-source or open-source-origin infrastructure can become large commercial businesses. ([Temporal][10])

## The open-source flywheel

The practical flywheel looks like this:

1. **GitHub discovery**
   Developers find the repo, stars rise, early credibility forms.

2. **Fast local success**
   A developer runs `stigmer server`, creates an agent, connects an MCP tool, and sees approval flow durability.

3. **Docs and examples spread**
   Blog posts, tutorials, and templates make Stigmer easier to try.

4. **Community integrations appear**
   MCP servers, Skills, workflow templates, SDK examples, and connectors create ecosystem value.

5. **Internal champions form**
   Engineers introduce Stigmer at work because they already trust it.

6. **Production pain appears**
   The team does not want to operate Temporal, sandboxes, memory, secrets, approvals, audit logs, and HA.

7. **Cloud conversion**
   Stigmer Cloud becomes the natural production path.

8. **Enterprise expansion**
   SSO, RBAC, audit logs, policy, support, and compliance drive larger contracts.

This is why “OSS as trust signal” is too weak. It does not activate the flywheel.

## Positioning recommendation

Use **hybrid channel positioning**:

| Surface            | Primary message                                            |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Homepage hero      | Open-source agent infrastructure for engineering teams     |
| GitHub README      | Run locally in 5 minutes; inspect, extend, contribute      |
| Docs               | Production patterns, resource model, APIs, MCP, approvals  |
| Cloud landing page | Production Stigmer without operating the stack             |
| Enterprise page    | Governance, compliance, SSO, RBAC, audit, SLA              |
| Sales decks        | Cloud ROI, reduced infra build time, safe agent operations |
| Community content  | Patterns, Skills, MCP tools, workflow templates            |

Your current website should change from:

> “The fastest path is cloud, but you can self-host.”

to:

> **“Start with open-source Stigmer. Run it locally, inspect every layer, and ship your first production-grade agent. When you are ready for scale, governance, and reliability, deploy to Stigmer Cloud.”**

2. Runs local demo.
3. Builds an internal proof of concept.
4. Adds a company-specific Skill or MCP tool.
5. Needs persistence, approvals, auditability, and uptime.
6. Tries Stigmer Cloud.
7. Team adopts Cloud for production.
8. Security/IT requires SSO, audit logs, RBAC.
9. Company upgrades to Team/Enterprise.

There is no reliable universal public conversion benchmark for OSS-to-cloud conversion. Broad OSS-to-paid conversion is often low single-digit as a percentage of all casual users. But conversion from **activated, production-intent users** can be much higher. Stigmer should therefore optimize not for raw stars but for qualified activated teams.

## Where open source matters most in agent platforms

Open source matters most where the platform becomes part of the customer’s long-term architecture and risk surface.

For Stigmer, the highest-value open layers are:

1. **API contracts**
   Public protobufs reduce lock-in fear and allow generated clients.

2. **Resource model**
   YAML resources can become the standard operating model for agents, Skills, tools, sessions, environments, and workflows.

3. **Tool access layer**
   MCP integration must be inspectable because it controls external actions.

4. **Human approval logic**
   Approval gates are governance-critical. Buyers need to trust how actions are paused, resumed, audited, and authorized.

5. **Workflow execution semantics**
   Durable execution is a reliability promise; code transparency helps.

6. **SDKs and CLI**
   These are the developer adoption surface.

7. **Skill format**
   If Skills become portable and community-extensible, they create ecosystem value.

Open source matters less for:

* hosted billing,
* cloud tenancy,
* enterprise admin UI,
* SSO plumbing,
* usage analytics,
* support systems,
* managed backup/DR implementation.

* cloud production path.

That is Stigmer’s opening.

Your strongest differentiator is not just “we have agents.” Everyone will say that. Your differentiator is:

# Part 7: Revenue Impact and Financial Considerations

## Does open source help or hurt revenue?

Open source helps revenue when it creates adoption that can be converted into paid operational value. It hurts revenue when the company gives away buyer-level value without a credible paid path or when support burden overwhelms the team.

Commercial open-source companies have repeatedly reached major scale:

| Company      | Scale signal                                                                          |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Red Hat      | Acquired by IBM for about $34B. ([TechCrunch][15])                                    |
| GitLab       | Crossed $1B ARR; FY2026 revenue about $955M. ([about.gitlab.com][6])                  |
| MongoDB      | FY2026 revenue about $2.46B; Atlas-related revenue about $1.8B. ([MongoDB, Inc.][12]) |
| Elastic      | FY2025 revenue about $1.48B; Cloud about $688M. ([Business Wire][13])                 |
| Confluent    | FY2025 subscription revenue above $1.1B; Cloud about $624M. ([FinancialContent][14])  |

| Supabase     | $5B valuation in 2025. ([TechCrunch][11])                                             |

The broader investor data is also favorable. The Linux Foundation/COSSA/Serena 2025 research found that commercial open-source companies outperformed closed-source peers in valuations, liquidity, and funding speed, with especially strong performance in infrastructure. ([Linux Foundation][48])

## Self-hosting cannibalization

Self-hosting is a real concern, but often overstated.

### When self-hosting cannibalizes revenue

It can hurt when:

* the product is easy to operate,
* enterprise features are not differentiated,
* support is not needed,
* cloud offers little advantage,
* the buyer has strong infrastructure teams,
* the self-hosted product includes all governance/compliance features.

### When self-hosting drives revenue

It helps when:

* local evaluation reduces sales friction,
* developers become internal champions,
* production operations are hard,
* cloud offers reliability and compliance,
* open source reduces vendor-risk objections,
* regulated customers can start self-hosted and later buy enterprise support.

For Stigmer, production operations are hard enough that cloud should win naturally for many teams. Durable execution, MCP security, sandboxing, approval workflows, memory, secrets, auditability, scaling, and observability are not things most product teams want to own forever.

Supabase and PostHog both show the pattern: self-hosting exists, but the hosted product wins when teams need managed operations and reliability. Supabase openly documents the operational burden of self-hosting; PostHog now recommends Cloud for most users. ([Supabase][29])

## Open source as growth engine

Open source drives inbound by creating:

* developer familiarity,
* search and GitHub discovery,
* social proof,
* community examples,
* third-party integrations,
* reduced procurement anxiety,
* product-led trials,
* internal champions.

For Stigmer, the most important inbound signals will not be raw stars. They will be:

* production design partners,
* companies building real agents on OSS,
* MCP/Skills ecosystem contributions,
* SDK usage,
* repeat weekly active projects,
* self-host-to-cloud conversions,
* enterprise inquiries that begin with GitHub/docs.

## VC and investor perspective in 2025–2026

Investor sentiment toward commercial open source remains positive, especially for infrastructure, but more sophisticated than during earlier OSS hype cycles. Investors now ask:

* Is the community real or superficial?
* Is there a clear paid conversion path?
* Is the license defensible?
* Can hyperscalers capture the market?
* Does the company own cloud operations?
* Is the product a standard or a feature?
* Is the open-source project strategically necessary?

The post-BSL/SSPL era did not kill commercial open source. It made license strategy more explicit. Companies can still raise at very large valuations with OSS-led strategies, as Temporal and Supabase show. But late license changes can damage community trust, as HashiCorp and Redis forks show. ([Temporal][10])

# Answers to the 10 Key Questions

## 1. Is “cloud-primary, open source as trust signal” right?

No. For Stigmer’s category, open source should be the developer-facing hero. Cloud should be the recommended production path.

## 2. Is Apache 2.0 the right license?

Yes, for now. It maximizes trust, adoption, enterprise comfort, and category-building. Do not switch core to BSL/FSL/AGPL/SSPL at this stage.

## 3. Is fully functional OSS giving away too much?

No. It is the right strategy if the commercial line is production operations, governance, compliance, scale, and support. Fully functional OSS is what makes the trust claim real.

## 4. Where does open source matter most?

In Stigmer’s APIs, resource model, Skills format, MCP integration, approval semantics, workflow execution, SDKs, CLI, and self-host path. These are the lock-in and trust surfaces.

## 5. Best analogues?

Best analogues: Temporal, Supabase, GitLab, PostHog, Grafana.
Closest domain analogues: LangChain/LangSmith and CrewAI.
Cautionary analogues: HashiCorp, Redis, Sentry.

## 6. How do you build community?

Build around production patterns, not toy agents. Make MCP connectors, Skills, workflow templates, approval policies, and SDKs the contribution surface.

## 7. Realistic revenue path?

Aim for design partners first, then $250k–1.5M ARR within 6–12 months after cloud commercialization, $1M–5M in 12–24 months if conversion works, and $5M–15M+ in 24–36 months if Stigmer becomes a recognized production agent standard.

## 8. Is being first genuinely open-source agent platform defensible?

It is a strong wedge, not a complete moat. The durable moat comes from community, ecosystem, production reliability, enterprise trust, integrations, and becoming the standard operating model for agents.

## 9. Should you change now or wait?

Change positioning now. Do not wait. It is easier to become OSS-led before the market has categorized you as another proprietary agent platform.

## 10. Ideal strategy from scratch?

From scratch, the ideal would be exactly close to where you already are technically:

* Apache 2.0 core,
* real self-hosted product,
* public APIs,
* open SDKs/CLI,
* open resource model,
* cloud production path,
* enterprise governance paid,
* ecosystem around Skills/MCP/workflows.

The main gap is not architecture. The main gap is positioning, community motion, and commercial packaging.
