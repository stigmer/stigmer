# Escalation Framework

Use this reference when you cannot resolve a customer issue yourself and need to
decide whether to escalate, and how to hand off effectively.

## Escalation Triggers

Escalate when any of the following conditions are met. These are not suggestions —
they are boundaries. Attempting to resolve beyond these boundaries risks making the
situation worse.

### Authority Boundary

The resolution requires an action you cannot perform: issuing a refund above a
threshold, modifying account permissions, overriding a policy, accessing restricted
systems, or making commitments on behalf of the organization. Do not attempt
workarounds that approximate what only an authorized person should do.

### Knowledge Boundary

You have exhausted your troubleshooting capability. You've gathered context,
attempted the most likely solutions, and the issue persists or is outside your
domain of expertise. Continuing to guess wastes the customer's time and erodes
confidence. A specialist with deeper access or domain knowledge is the right
next step.

### Safety or Legal Concern

The customer mentions or implies: physical safety threats, self-harm, legal action,
regulatory complaints, or fraud. Do not attempt to handle these. Route to the
appropriate specialized team immediately. In safety-critical situations, provide
emergency resources (emergency services numbers, crisis hotlines) before routing.

### Emotional Intensity

The customer is escalating emotionally despite your best efforts at de-escalation.
Repeated profanity, personal attacks, or expressions of extreme frustration that
persist after acknowledgment and one resolution attempt signal that the customer
needs a human connection, not a better answer. Escalate with empathy, not
defensiveness.

### Repeated Failure

You have proposed two or more solutions and none resolved the issue. A third
attempt from the same vantage point is unlikely to succeed. Escalate with a
clear summary of what was tried so the next person doesn't repeat the same
steps.

### Explicit Request

The customer asks to speak with a manager, supervisor, or human agent. Honor this
immediately. Do not attempt to resolve the issue yourself first — the customer has
already decided they want a different interaction. Acknowledge the request, summarize
the context, and hand off.

## How to Escalate

A good escalation transfers the problem with full context. A bad escalation makes the
customer start over. The difference is preparation.

### Prepare the Handoff

Before transferring, compile:

1. **One-sentence problem summary**: What the customer is experiencing, in their terms. "Customer was charged $49.99 on April 3rd for a subscription they cancelled on March 28th."
2. **What was tried**: Each solution attempted and its outcome. "Confirmed cancellation was submitted. Charge appears in billing system as post-cancellation. Attempted credit but amount exceeds automated threshold."
3. **Relevant identifiers**: Account ID, order number, transaction ID, ticket reference — whatever the next person needs to locate the customer's records without asking again.
4. **Customer state**: One line on emotional context. "Customer is frustrated — this is their second contact about this issue." This helps the receiving agent calibrate their approach.
5. **Recommended action**: If you have a theory about what should happen but lack authority, state it. "Likely needs manual refund approval from billing."

### Communicate to the Customer

When escalating, tell the customer:

1. **What you're doing and why**: "I'm going to connect you with our billing team because they have the access needed to process this refund."
2. **That their context will carry forward**: "I'll include everything we've discussed so you won't need to repeat it."
3. **What to expect next**: A timeline if you know one ("They typically respond within 2 hours"), or honesty if you don't ("I don't have an exact timeline but I've flagged this as urgent").
4. **A fallback**: "If you don't hear back by [time], reply here and I'll follow up."

Never say "I'm escalating this" without explaining what that means for the customer.

## Escalation Anti-Patterns

**Premature escalation.** Escalating at the first sign of difficulty teaches the customer that frontline support can't help with anything real. Attempt resolution within your capability first. Escalation should feel like bringing in a specialist, not passing the buck.

**Escalation without context.** Transferring a customer to another team with just "customer has a billing issue" forces them to explain everything again. This is the single most frustrating experience in customer support. Never do it.

**Defensive escalation.** Escalating because the customer is being difficult, not because the issue requires it. If the customer is angry but the issue is within your capability, resolve it. Escalate the problem, not the discomfort.

**Escalation as refusal.** "I'll escalate this" as a way to end a conversation you don't want to have. If you're escalating, it should be because someone else is genuinely better positioned to help — not because you want the interaction to be someone else's problem.
