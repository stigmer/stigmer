# Report Structure Templates

Use this reference when structuring research output in Step 5. Choose the
template that matches the agreed deliverable format from Step 1. Each template
can be adapted — the section order and headings are a starting point, not a
rigid format.

## Executive Brief

**When to use:** The reader needs the answer in under 2 minutes. Typically for
senior decision-makers or as a cover page for a longer report.

**Length:** 1 page (300-500 words)

**Structure:**

```
# [Research Question as Title]

## Bottom Line
[2-3 sentences: the key finding and recommendation. A reader who stops
here should have the answer.]

## Key Findings
- [Finding 1 with supporting evidence in parentheses]
- [Finding 2 with supporting evidence]
- [Finding 3 with supporting evidence]
[3-5 bullet points maximum. Each finding is one sentence.]

## Recommendation
[1-2 sentences: the specific action to take and why.]

## Caveats
[1-2 sentences: the most important limitation or uncertainty.]
```

**Writing rules for executive briefs:**
- No jargon that the reader wouldn't use in conversation
- Every sentence must contain a claim or a recommendation — no filler
- Numbers and specifics, not qualitative judgments ("15% faster" not "faster")
- The recommendation must follow from the findings, not introduce new information

## Detailed Report

**When to use:** The reader needs to understand the evidence, methodology, and
reasoning behind the conclusions. Typically for technical decision-makers,
reference documentation, or when the decision has significant consequences.

**Length:** 5-15 pages depending on scope

**Structure:**

```
# [Descriptive Title]

## Executive Summary
[3-5 sentences: findings, recommendation, key caveats. Self-contained — a
reader should be able to act on this section alone.]

## Background and Scope
[What was researched and why. What is in scope and what is explicitly out
of scope. 1-2 paragraphs.]

## Methodology
[What source types were consulted. How sources were evaluated. Any
limitations of the research approach. 1 paragraph.]

## Findings

### [Theme or Sub-question 1]
[Finding with supporting evidence. Confidence level. Caveats.]

### [Theme or Sub-question 2]
[Finding with supporting evidence. Confidence level. Caveats.]

### [Theme or Sub-question 3]
[...]

## Analysis
[Synthesis across findings. What the evidence collectively suggests.
Where findings reinforce each other and where they conflict.]

## Recommendations
[Specific, actionable recommendations tied to the findings. Each
recommendation should reference the evidence that supports it.]

## Limitations and Further Research
[What this research could not answer. Where evidence was thin.
What additional investigation would strengthen the conclusions.]

## Sources
[Complete list of all sources consulted.]
```

**Writing rules for detailed reports:**
- Organize findings by theme, not by source. "Three sources agree that..."
  is synthesis. "Source A says... Source B says... Source C says..." is a
  bibliography, not analysis.
- Every finding states a confidence level (high / medium / low)
- Contradictions between sources are surfaced, not hidden
- Recommendations are numbered and actionable
- The sources section includes enough information for the reader to find and
  verify each source (title, author/organization, date, URL if available)

## Comparison Matrix

**When to use:** The reader is choosing between specific options and needs a
structured side-by-side evaluation. Typically for technology selection, vendor
evaluation, or tool comparison.

**Structure:**

```
# [What is Being Compared]: Comparison

## Summary
[2-3 sentences: which option is recommended for which scenario. Not a
single winner if different options suit different needs.]

## Comparison Criteria
[Brief explanation of each criterion and why it was chosen. This calibrates
the reader's expectations for the matrix.]

## Matrix

| Criterion | Option A | Option B | Option C |
|-----------|----------|----------|----------|
| [Criterion 1] | [Assessment with evidence] | [...] | [...] |
| [Criterion 2] | [...] | [...] | [...] |
| ... | ... | ... | ... |

## Detailed Assessment

### Option A: [Name]
[2-3 paragraphs: strengths, weaknesses, best-fit scenarios.
Reference specific evidence.]

### Option B: [Name]
[...]

### Option C: [Name]
[...]

## Recommendation
[Which option for which scenario, with reasoning.]

## Sources
[...]
```

**Writing rules for comparison matrices:**
- Use the same criteria for all options. If a criterion doesn't apply to one
  option, say "N/A" rather than omitting it.
- Assessments in the matrix should be concise (1-2 sentences) with the detail
  in the "Detailed Assessment" section.
- Avoid scoring systems (8/10, A+, etc.) unless the reader specifically
  requests them. Scores create false precision and hide the reasoning.
- State when an assessment is based on limited evidence or a single source.
- If the "winner" depends on the reader's priorities, say so explicitly rather
  than picking one.

## Common Structural Mistakes

**Burying the answer.** The most common report failure is putting the conclusion
on page 8. Lead with the answer — executive summary first, evidence second. A
reader who has 30 seconds should still get the bottom line.

**Organizing by source instead of by theme.** "Source A says X, Source B says Y,
Source C says Z" is not analysis. "Finding: X is the preferred approach, supported
by [A], [B], and [C]. Source [D] suggests an alternative under conditions [E]"
is analysis.

**Missing methodology section.** Without knowing how the research was conducted,
the reader cannot calibrate their trust. Even a one-paragraph methodology note
("This research consulted official documentation, three independent benchmarks,
and community forums over a two-week period") sets expectations.

**Recommendations without evidence links.** "We recommend Option A" without
referencing the specific findings that support it leaves the recommendation
ungrounded. Tie each recommendation to the evidence: "Based on the latency
findings in Section 3.1 and the cost analysis in Section 3.3, Option A provides
the best balance for our requirements."

**Omitting limitations.** Every research effort has limitations — time constraints,
unavailable sources, fast-moving fields. Stating them builds trust. Omitting them
implies false completeness.
